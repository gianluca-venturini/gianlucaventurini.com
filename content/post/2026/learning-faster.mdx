With an AI tutor such as Claude, I now learn hard topics much faster. Before, I needed a few hours of books, classes and videos before I could work with the core ideas. Now I need a few minutes. In this post, I explain my method, using the [SpaceX Raptor engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Raptor) as an example.

Learning is a big part of how I spend my solo time. I enjoy learning about a wide range of topics, such as physics, math or economics. I love the feeling of being deeply engaged with the core ideas, the felling of minutes turning into hours without realizing the passage of time.

## How I used to learn

I'm always been a practical learner: I can read the same concept multiple times and barely remember it, but once I engage with it on a playful simulation it's permanently burned in my brain. Up to one year ago the fastest way I learned was watching just enough Youtube videos, online classes, books, blog posts or papers that I could code a simulation. I find all these methods effective, but requiring a lot of my attention and dedication, meaning I can only engage few times per week. This has changed drastically in the past year. Before, I had to build each simulation myself. Most of that time went into details that were not about the topic, such as how to draw a pipe or why my code had a bug. Now the tutor builds the simulation in a few minutes. I spend all my time on the core ideas: I change the simulation, ask a question, and see the result as fast as I can think of the next question.

In [a post from 2025](/posts/2025/assisted-coding), I wrote that LLMs make coding less interesting, because they do the thinking for me. Learning is different. The tutor does the work that is not the topic, and I keep the thinking.

## My method now

The method I'm now using is jumping directly to core ideas of the topic in a conversation with an AI tutor. I use Claude, but other AI tutors can do the same. I use the explainer style of [Bartosz Ciechanowski](https://ciechanow.ski), one of my favorite bloggers of all time. My favorite of his posts is [Mechanical Watch](https://ciechanow.ski/mechanical-watch/).

**Step 1.** Ask the tutor for the simplest possible explainer page. An explainer page is a single web page with text and diagrams. The tutor makes it for you (Claude calls it an [artifact](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/artifacts)). Let's supposed I want to learn how the Raptor engine works, I would start with a simple prompt like:

> How does a rocket engine works? Let's take the Raptor Engine from Spacex. I want you to create the simplest possible explainer with a very simple figure, I'll guide you where to go from there.

![Simple diagram of a Raptor engine: methane and oxygen go through turbopumps, burn at high pressure, and the nozzle pushes the gas out fast to make thrust](</uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-engine-simple.png>)

**Step 2.** Once the list of components and relationship is clear, I expand.

> We need to make the diagram a little more complete with all the main components, for example how is the nozzle prevented from melting? How are the fluids injected in the combustion chamber? We'll do a simple 2D visualization complete with all the real components.

The tutor then adds [all the main components](/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-engine-anatomy.html#eng) of the full-flow cycle: the fuel and oxygen pumps, the turbines, the two preburners, the main injector, the cooling jacket, the combustion chamber and the nozzle.

<Embed src="/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-engine-anatomy.html#eng" title="2D diagram of the Raptor engine with all the main components" />

**Step 3.** The next step is the most rewarding: keep expanding whatever sparks my curiosity. I add a comment to any part of the page, and the tutor answers it. There's no set amount of time here, only the occasional subscription token limit stops me.

![A comment on the fuel-rich preburner in the explainer page, sent to Claude. It will quiz on this later](</uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-comment.png>)

I keep going until the explainer page [answers all my questions](/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-engine-anatomy.html). Often I start tangents, for example analyzing how a specific component works: [raptor engine turbopump](/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-fuel-turbopump.html).

**Step 4.** When I think I understand the topic, I ask the tutor to [quiz me](/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-quiz.html). The quiz finds the parts that I only think I know. Before, a working simulation told me that I understood a topic. Now the quiz does that job.

> Quiz me on the Raptor engine. Ask one question at a time and wait for my answer. Do not tell me the answer before I try.

<Embed src="/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-quiz.html" title="Raptor engine quiz with fifteen questions" height="auto" />

**Step 5.** Furthermore, now that the tutor is really good at 3D modeling I often repackage the simplified concepts [into a realistic 3D model](/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-3d-astra.html) in order to appreciate how all the concepts I learned fit together as physical object that I can now understand (and 3D print if I wanted to).

<Embed src="/uploads/2026/learning-faster/raptor-3d-astra.html?embed=1&select=Oxygen%20turbopump,Methane%20turbopump&flow=1" title="3D model of the Raptor engine with the two turbopumps selected and the fluid flow on" />

## Conclusions

The example I presented is inherently visual but this method works well for any hard topic. I find this activity extremely effective in teaching me complex concepts. What I like the most of it is that it's extremely addictive in all the possible good ways: requires very little activation energy, got a curiosity, make this 30min trip to the airport a learning session!. I've never experienced that much engagement with an arbitrary topic, I'm now learning as fast as I can think about new topics.
