Three Branches, One Constitution

The framers split federal power three ways, then gave each branch tools to block the other two. No branch can act alone for long. That friction is the design: it forces bargaining, slows rash decisions, and makes it hard for any one faction to take control.

“Ambition must be made to counteract ambition.” — James Madison, Federalist No. 51
Part 1

Who does what

Separation of powers: each branch gets its own job, its own people, and its own way of being chosen. Different terms and different electorates mean they rarely all shift at once.

Article I

Legislative · Congress

Job
Writes the laws and controls the money.
Who
House: 435 members, 2-year terms, seats by population. Senate: 100 members, 6-year staggered terms, 2 per state.
  • Pass bills, tax and spend
  • Declare war, regulate commerce
  • House impeaches; Senate tries
  • Senate confirms nominees, ratifies treaties
Article II

Executive · President

Job
Carries out and enforces the laws.
Who
President and Vice President, 4-year terms via the Electoral College; two-term limit (22nd Amendment). Runs the federal departments and agencies.
  • Sign or veto bills
  • Commander in chief
  • Nominate judges and officials
  • Negotiate treaties, grant pardons
Article III

Judicial · Courts

Job
Interprets the laws and the Constitution in actual cases.
Who
Supreme Court (9 justices, a number set by Congress) plus lower federal courts. Judges serve “during good behaviour”, in practice for life.
  • Decide cases and controversies
  • Judicial review: void unconstitutional laws and acts
  • Settle disputes between states

Beyond the three branches, two more checks sit underneath everything: voters, who replace the House every two years, a third of the Senate every two years, and the President every four; and federalism, which keeps many powers with the states. Constitutional amendments need two-thirds of both chambers and three-quarters of the states, so the people and states hold the final word even over the Supreme Court.
